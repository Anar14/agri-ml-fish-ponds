## 1. Target leakage (most important)

**Old:** `X = df.drop('Fish_Weight(g)', axis=1)`, which kept `Fish_Length(cm)`, `Year`, `Month`, `Day` and `Population` as features.

**New:** `FEATURES = [Temperature, Dissolved Oxygen, PH, Nitrate, Population, days_since_start]` (notebooks 04 and 05).

**Why:** length and weight are measured together on sampling days, and each length maps to exactly one weight (pond 10: 19 lengths, 19 weights). The model could look the weight up from the length. That is why XGBoost reached R² 0.99998 and the classifiers reached 99.99 % accuracy. Notebook 04 now prints this one-to-one check.

## 2. Train/test split

**Old:** `train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.3, random_state=42)` and `KFold(shuffle=True)` on about 1 million rows of 20-second readings.

**New:** readings are averaged to one row per pond per day (`pond_day_table()`), then evaluated with `GroupKFold(groups=pond_id)`.

**Why:** with a random split, readings seconds apart from the same pond and day end up in both train and test, so the model memorises the day. Fish weight is also repeated on thousands of rows between sampling days. Grouping by pond tests the model on ponds it has never seen.

## 3. Baselines

**Old:** none.

**New:** `DummyRegressor` (predict the mean) and a linear model on `days_since_start` only (04), and `DummyClassifier` (05).

**Why:** fish grow over time whatever the water quality. The gap between the "days only" baseline and the sensor models shows what the sensors add.

## 4. Date parsing

**Old:**
```python
df['Date'] = df['created_at'].apply(lambda x: pd.to_datetime(x, errors='coerce', dayfirst=False).date())
```
**New:** `parse_timestamps()` in `src/pond_pipeline.py` parses `2021-06-25 15:48:59` with an explicit ISO format and `7/11/2021` as day-first.

**Why:** slash dates in the files are day-first. With `dayfirst=False`, `7/11/2021` became 11 July instead of 7 November, and `5/12/2021` became 12 May. In pond 10 alone, 164 rows landed in May, before the experiment started. `Month` and `Day` were wrong for those rows.

## 5. Valid-range thresholds

**Old:** different rules in different places.

| Where | Temperature | Dissolved oxygen | Nitrate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `Before preprocessing` (whole data) | < 0 or > 30 | < 3 | /100 > 25 |
| `Before preprocessing` (per pond, `wrong_values_per_pond.csv`) | < 20 or > 30 | **< 5** | /100 > 25 |
| `Wrong_Values` count vs cleaning | < 20 or > 30 | **< 5** vs **< 3** | /100 > 25 |
| `Preprocessing and Regression` cleaning | < 20 or > 30 | < 3 | > 25 (only ponds 6 and 12 divided by 100) |

**New:** one `VALID_RANGES` dict in `src/pond_pipeline.py`, used both to count wrong values (01) and to clean (03): temperature 20–30 °C, pH 6.5–8.5, DO ≥ 5 mg/L, turbidity 0–50 NTU, nitrate 0–25 mg/L, ammonia 0–0.1 mg/L. These are the ranges the thesis states (section on invalid values). **Later change (dissolved oxygen):** the 5 mg/L limit is kept for *reporting* (the fault tables) but not for *cleaning*. Readings below 5 mg/L are real low-oxygen conditions, so removing and imputing them pushed every pond's cleaned minimum to exactly 5.00 mg/L and hid the conditions that slow growth. Cleaning now removes only impossible oxygen values: exactly 0 mg/L or less (sensor dropout; pond 10's sample is more than half zeros) and above 20 mg/L (`CLEANING_RANGES`). The thesis method section needs one sentence changed to match.

**Why:** the wrong-value tables in the thesis (Table 3, DO > 5) did not match what was actually removed before modelling (DO < 3 set to NaN). This is also why the thesis's Table 5 text says DO ranges "from 3 to 44.98 mg/L (above the survival threshold of 5 mg/L)". The code now follows the thesis text. Set `CLEANING_RANGES[DO] = (3.0, None)` to reproduce the old cleaning, or `(5.0, None)` for the thesis text.

## 6. Units and column names

**Old:** columns named `Dissolved Oxygen(g/ml)`, `Ammonia(g/ml)`, `Nitrate(g/ml)` in the notebooks, but `(mg/L)` in `Iotpond10.csv` and `IotPond_new.csv`. The notebooks fail with `KeyError` on the included CSV.

**New:** `load_pond()` renames every `(g/ml)` to `(mg/L)` on load.

## 7. Nitrate scale

**Old:** `df6['Nitrate(g/ml)'] / 100` and `df12[...] / 100` only. The included `Iotpond10.csv` has nitrate up to 2,473 (580 of 620 rows > 25), so the `> 25` rule would remove almost all of it.

**New:** if a pond's median nitrate is above 25, it is divided by 100 (`load_pond()`).

## 8. Imputation (cleaning)

**Old:**
```python
knn_imputer = KNNImputer(n_neighbors=5)
df1.iloc[:, :] = knn_imputer.fit_transform(df1)
```
This ran on all columns, unscaled, including `Fish_Weight(g)`, `Fish_Length(cm)`, `Year`, `Month` and `Day`.

**New:** `polynomial_impute()` in `src/pond_pipeline.py`: each sensor column is predicted from the other sensor columns with a degree-2 polynomial regression, per pond. Imputed values are clipped to the range observed in that pond, because a polynomial can extrapolate far outside the data (an unclipped test gave oxygen above 3,000 mg/L). `clean_pond(..., method="knn")` still gives a scaled KNN version (`knn_impute_scaled()`), matching the thesis.

**Why:** (a) using the target to fill features leaks it into the inputs. (b) The fish columns and dates are not sensor faults and do not need imputing. (c) In the corrected comparison (notebook 02, run on the full data, pond 6) polynomial regression had the lowest error: mean NRMSE 0.743 (DO 0.872, nitrate 0.709, pH 0.637, temperature 0.753), against iterative 0.789, KNN 0.975 and the mean 1.000. This reverses the thesis conclusion that KNN was better, which came from scoring the two methods on different cells (section 10).

## 9. Rows with no sensor data

**Old:** pond 6's trailing rows (dates such as `18/12/2021` with every sensor empty) were kept, and KNN filled in all their sensor values.

**New:** rows where every sensor is missing are dropped in `load_pond()`.

## 10. Imputation comparison (`Comparison of Imputation methods.ipynb` → 02)

| Issue in the old version | New version |
| --- | --- |
| KNN MSE was computed over all cells, 90 % of them unchanged (error 0); polynomial MSE only over masked cells, so KNN looked better | Every method is scored only on the hidden cells |
| KNN's "true values" were themselves KNN-imputed | True values are the observed readings before hiding |
| Masked rows could already be NaN | Only observed cells are hidden |
| No random seed | `np.random.default_rng(42)` |
| One MSE pooled across variables with different scales | RMSE, MAE and NRMSE (RMSE / std) per variable |
| KNN used 4 columns, polynomial used the others | All methods use the same 4 sensor columns |
| Only KNN and polynomial | Mean, KNN (scaled), Iterative (`max_iter=100`) and polynomial; the polynomial function is the same one the cleaning uses |

## 11. Classification labels

**Old:** `pd.cut(bins=5)` (equal-width) gave 730,139 vs 12,892 rows per class. The balanced `qcut` version was commented out, but the second set of results depended on it.

**New:** equal-width bins (`pd.cut(bins=5)`) as in the thesis, on the pond-day table, with the class edges printed; equal-frequency bins (`pd.qcut`) are run in the same notebook for comparison. `class_weight="balanced"` where supported. Metrics: accuracy, balanced accuracy, macro F1 and ROC AUC on out-of-pond predictions.

**Why the metrics changed:** the thesis chose equal-width bins because accuracy was 0.84 vs 0.67. With equal-width bins 73 % of rows are class 0, so accuracy mostly measures class 0. Macro F1 and balanced accuracy treat each class equally and are the fairer basis for that choice.

**Per-model detail (as in the thesis):** for every model (logistic regression, decision tree, random forest, XGBoost) and both binnings, notebook 05 shows a row-normalised confusion matrix, one-vs-rest ROC curves with the AUC of each class and the macro AUC, and a per-class table of precision, recall, F1 and AUC. Figures go to `results/figures/confusion_matrices_<binning>.png` and `roc_curves_<binning>.png`, tables to `results/classification_report_<binning>.csv` and `results/classification_results_<binning>.csv`. An earlier version of this repo showed these only for the best model.

**Also:** the old PCA pipelines were removed. PCA hid the length leak, which is why those scores (67–84 %) looked "realistic" next to the 99.99 % without PCA.

## 4a. Day/month order chosen per pond

**Found after the first full run:** ponds 7 and 8 showed fish weight jumping between about 0 g and 800 g within days, over a span of more than 300 days. Their date strings are not ISO, and parsing them with `format="mixed"` lets pandas guess the order row by row: a date is read day-first when that is possible and month-first otherwise. So 6 March and 3 June get mixed up, and the rows land in the wrong order.

**New:** `parse_timestamps(values, dayfirst)` reads every non-ISO date in a column with one fixed order. `choose_timestamps()` tries `created_at` and `Date`, day-first and month-first, and keeps the reading that parses the most rows and keeps the file (sorted by `entry_id`) in time order. `timestamp_report()` in notebook 01 shows the choice and the date span for every pond. In a test with month-first dates, the old parser spread 5 months of data from January to December, out of order; the new one keeps the true 5 months in order.

**Also added:** `weight_by_day()` in notebook 01 lists the days where one pond has several weights or a drop in weight (pond 11 showed single-day spikes, see 4c).

**Date coverage (later fix):** on the real data ponds 7 and 8 got a date for only 63% and 85% of rows. The gap filling from the other date column now lets that column use its own day/month order, the parser also reads two-digit years, AM/PM times, `T`-separated ISO times and fractional seconds, and `parsed` is measured only on rows that hold sensor data. `timestamp_report()` adds `unparsed rows with data` (rows dropped because no date could be read), and `unparsed_timestamps()` in notebook 01 lists the date strings that still fail.

## 4c. Stale fish weights between sampling days

**Found on the real data:** pond 11 logs a new weight about every two weeks (588.8 g, 625.9 g, 644.4 g, 701.0 g), but all other rows keep an old 281.2 g, so the weight spikes on each sampling day and falls back the next day. On a sampling day both weights appear, so the daily mean mixed them.

**New:** `fix_stale_weights()`, applied by `load_pond()`, gives every row of a day the highest weight logged that day and keeps the latest sampling weight until a higher one is logged, because the average fish in a pond does not shrink. Length is taken from the same row. `timestamp_report()` shows how many rows changed per pond (`stale weights replaced`), and the notebook 01 table compares logged weights with the weight used. `load_pond(..., fix_weights=False)` gives the weights as logged. **Pond 11 restock:** the last weight, 303.0 g on 2022-02-04, is a new batch of fish stocked that day (confirmed), not a stale value. Only a few days of the new batch were logged, too few to model, so `load_pond()` leaves out pond 11 rows from 2022-02-04 on (`RESTOCK_DATES` in `src/pond_pipeline.py`). `timestamp_report()` shows how many rows were left out (`rows after restock dropped`). Without this, the new batch would either be raised to 701 g or appear as the first batch shrinking.

## 4b. Timestamps with a UTC offset

Some rows carry an offset such as `+01:00`. Mixing them with offset-free rows raised a tz-aware vs tz-naive error. `parse_timestamps()` now strips the offset so every timestamp is local, offset-free time.

## 4d. Checking which cleaning made the processed files

Notebooks 04–06 read `data/processed/pond_day.csv`, written by notebook 03. If notebook 03 runs in a kernel that imported an older `src/pond_pipeline.py`, Python keeps the old code and the processed file is rewritten with old results. ## 4e. Effect of the fixes on the results

After the date, weight and oxygen fixes, a time-only linear model explains 89 % of weight variance on unseen ponds (R² 0.890) and no sensor model beats it. Before the fixes the same evaluation gave 0.41 for time only and 0.53 with sensors, because scrambled dates (ponds 7 and 8) and stale weights (pond 11) hid the growth curve. The README results section has the final tables.

## 14. Findings from the earlier draft notebooks

These drafts were found later. None of them changes the fixes above; several support them.

- **Your own drafts already showed the leak.** In `IotPond_cc.ipynb`, 5-fold CV *without* shuffling (folds roughly follow ponds, because the data is stacked pond by pond) gave XGBoost R² of 0.95, 0.98, 0.92, **0.56** and 0.81 (mean 0.84). With `shuffle=True` the same model scored 0.99998. The difference is the leak. Your note in that notebook says the model "memorises".
- **Feature importance confirms it.** Random forest importance in `IotPond_2.ipynb`: `Fish_Length` 0.991, every other feature under 0.004. Decision tree: `Fish_Length` 0.995.
- **More leaking features.** `pond_id` and `Year` were also inputs in some versions (linear regression coefficient for `Year`: 288).
- **Nitrate scaling differs between drafts.** `IotPond.ipynb` divides nitrate by 100 for **all** ponds; `Preprocessing and Regression.ipynb` only for ponds 6 and 12. So different versions of `IotPond_new.csv` exist. The new code checks each pond's scale instead.
- **Single-column KNN imputation is mean imputation.** `IotPond_2.ipynb` imputed pH with `KNNImputer` on the pH column alone. With no other column, every missing row gets the column mean. Its MSE (4.53) is therefore the error of mean imputation. Notebook 02 now reports mean imputation as its own baseline.
- **Resampling before cross-validation.** `IotPond-Classification.ipynb` applied SMOTE and undersampling to the whole dataset before `cross_val_predict` (93 % accuracy). Synthetic samples built from test rows then sit in training folds. Not used in the thesis; not carried over. The new code uses `class_weight="balanced"` instead.
- **Outlier removal on the training set only.** `IotPond_cc.ipynb`: removing IQR outliers from the training rows dropped the random forest's test R² to 0.56; capping them restored 0.99997. This again shows the score depends on exact length values, not on water quality.
- **`IotPond-Copy1.ipynb`**: ponds 11 and 12 filtered with pond 1's mask (`df11.loc[df1['Temperature (C)'] < 0, ...]`). That draft was not used for the thesis.
- **`Statistics_after_imputation.csv`** (basis of thesis Table 5): oxygen minimum is 3.0 mg/L, not 5 as the table states; `Year` reaches 2022 and `Month` 1, i.e. date-only rows from January 2022 whose sensor values were entirely imputed; `pond_id` only reaches 9, so ponds 10–12 were missing or relabelled in that file. The new loader drops rows with no sensor data and keeps the real pond numbers.
- **`Visualization.ipynb`**: plotted raw means (including −127 °C and `inf` ammonia) of temperature, pH, oxygen, nitrate, weight, length and population as bars on one shared axis, so the units cannot be compared; the median chart was titled "means"; it reads a `Statistics.xlsx` that is not in the folder. `06_visualization.ipynb` uses cleaned data, medians with interquartile ranges, and one panel per variable.
